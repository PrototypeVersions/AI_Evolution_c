Reproduce, or live forever?

Real neural networks learn in a world that never stops getting harder. Every one gets exactly the same computing budget. Some live forever. Others make successors, by copying themselves, by teaching a child, or by passing on only their learning settings. Which way of living keeps a mind improving?

Why this question

Animals age and have children. Machines don't have to.

Evolution settled on bodies that wear out and offspring that start fresh. A digital mind can be copied exactly, taught, retrained or kept running forever. Nobody knows which of these is the better way for a learning system to accumulate ability over a long time. That question matters more as AI systems are trained on top of earlier AI systems.

01

Long-lived networks stiffen

Networks trained for a long time on changing problems gradually lose the ability to learn new ones (Dohare et al., Nature, 2024). Starting over cures that, but throws away what was learned.

02

Inheritance has channels

A successor can inherit the weights themselves, the behaviour (by being taught), or only the settings that shape how it learns. Each channel passes on something different, and each costs something different.

03

Equal compute makes it fair

Every strategy spends exactly the same number of operations per era. Teaching a child uses the parent's time, and a population splits one budget four ways. Nothing gets free compute.

The world

A staircase of skills that never ends

Each input is 32 random bits, ±1. A skill is the parity of a few of those bits: whether an even or odd number of them are −1. The world starts with four 2-bit skills. Every few eras a new skill appears, made by adding one bit to a skill that already exists.

Big parities are almost impossible to learn from scratch, but easy if you already know the smaller one they grew from (the "staircase" effect, Abbe et al., 2022). In our check, fresh networks failed at 5- and 6-bit skills in 5 of 6 tries, while networks that climbed one bit at a time needed 75 to 300 steps per step. So knowledge has to accumulate, and forgetting or losing the ability to learn is costly.

Each era, the task is to tell apart four combinations of two skills. One era is 800 training steps of 32 examples, the same for everyone.

The contestants

Nine ways to live

Seven life histories were fixed in advance. Two more, with a longer childhood, were added after the pilot showed that a short lesson passes on very little; they are tested separately. A lifespan is how many eras pass between births (5 or 20).
